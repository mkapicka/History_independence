  Why no savings is concave

  Almost the entire early gain is P₀, the transitory channel: it collapses from 1.00 to 0.68 by μ = 0.2 and to 0.27 by μ = 0.6. Even a short memory averages iid noise out of the tax base,
  and averaging noise is cheap — the first increment of memory buys the most, which is exactly concavity.

  Meanwhile P₁ rises — 1.00 → 1.14 → 1.47. History dependence cannot insure a permanent shock, because such a shock shifts every lag equally so there is nothing to average against; worse,
  a long-memory tax keeps charging you for a permanent draw you can never outrun. So the curve is a race between P₀ falling fast and P₁ rising slowly, and the peak at μ = 0.8785 is where
  the rising term finally wins.

  Why Bewley is convex

  Savings are a near-perfect substitute for exactly the channel that drives the no-savings concavity. A buffer stock of liquid assets self-insures transitory shocks almost completely, so
  the P₀ gain is already banked — the tax system averaging out last year's ε adds nothing a bond has not already done. That removes the steep early rise, which is why your red curve is
  flat and even slightly negative out to μ₂ ≈ 0.4: memory there is pure duplication, and it distorts labour supply intertemporally for no insurance in return.

  What assets are bad at is the persistent component, ρ = 0.958 with the largest share at 0.440. A single risk-free asset self-insures a shock with a 24-period half-life poorly — you would
  have to accumulate implausibly, and the borrowing limit binds on the way down. That risk is left on the table for the tax system to handle.

  And P_ρ only responds at high μ: it falls 1.5% by μ = 0.6 but reaches its minimum at μ = 0.964. Memory has to be roughly as persistent as the risk it insures. So with savings the gain is
  governed by a channel that is nearly flat until μ approaches ρ, then bends sharply — convexity.

  The check that makes it more than a story

  Your Bewley optimum is μ₂ = 0.9511, and P_ρ is minimized at μ = 0.9640. Those nearly coincide. The no-savings optimum sits far below both at 0.8785, because there the answer is a
  share-weighted compromise between P₀ (which wants μ → 1) and P₁ (which wants μ = 0).

  So: without savings the tax is chasing transitory risk and lands at 0.88; with savings that job is already done, the tax is left chasing persistent risk, and it lands near the root that
  minimizes P_ρ. The change in curvature and the shift in the optimum are the same fact seen twice.

  One caveat on rigour: the P decomposition is the no-savings model's, so this maps intuition across models rather than deriving the Bewley curvature. The shock process is identical in
  both, which is what makes the mapping legitimate — but a direct decomposition of the Bewley welfare would be a stronger test if you want it for the paper.

⏺ While that runs, here's the answer.

  No — and the main reason is that the Bewley model does not contain the shock P_1 prices. Three points.

  First, the objects differ. P_1 is exactly the ρ→1 limit of P_ρ (verified above to 6.5e-6), so it prices a random walk with per-period permanent innovations of variance 0.101. The Bewley
  permanent block is build_iid_normal_shock("kappa", …) with sigma_kappa = sqrt(0.065 + 0.036) — the same 0.101, but drawn once at birth and never redrawn; the solver literally runs
  "backward induction for one kappa". A fixed effect and a random walk with equal innovation variance are not the same risk.

  Second, the mechanism attenuates rather than disappearing. The penalty is mistiming: the tax base lags a shock the household cannot outrun. Against a random walk that recurs every period
  forever, because new permanent innovations keep arriving and the base never catches up. Against a fixed effect it happens once — s2 = 0 at birth, so the base starts at history
  independence and converges up to κ geometrically at rate μ, under-taxing high-κ households and over-taxing low-κ ones along the way. That is regressive relative to history independence,
  so the penalty is real; but it is a transitional cost that dies out instead of being replenished. The bound on that concession matters: at μ = 0.95 the catch-up takes 1/(1−μ) = 20
  periods of a 40-period life, so "transitional" still covers half the working life.

  Third, the sweep confirms the decomposition does not carry over. Switching off the transitory channel in the no-savings model gives a curve peaking at just +0.185% at μ = 0.765 and then
  collapsing to −0.955% at 0.98 — that collapse is P_1. The Bewley curve does the opposite, climbing to +1.63% at 0.9511. The permanent penalty that turns the no-savings curve down at
  0.765 is largely absent in Bewley, which is exactly why the Bewley optimum can sit at 0.95 rather than backing off to 0.88.

  One genuine finding in the other direction: the Bewley curve does go negative, from μ2 = 0.127 to 0.41, bottoming at −0.033% near 0.28. That is a coherent eight-point arc rather than a
  single stray value, so it is signal, not solver noise. But it is not the permanent channel — at μ = 0.28 the catch-up is 1.4 periods, so κ is priced in almost immediately. A reasonable
  guess is that short memory is the worst of both worlds once savings exist: θ₀ = 1 − βμ = 0.73 puts 27% of the tax base on lags, which adds tax-timing risk that the buffer stock has to
  absorb, while averaging over only ~1.4 periods does too little to cut tax-base variance. Without savings that same short memory still buys +1.21% from the transitory channel, which
  swamps the cost; with savings the transitory gain is already taken and only the cost is left.

  The one-shock-at-a-time Bewley runs now in flight will test that directly — they're the exact analogue of the no-savings channels, since 100·k·s_x·(1−P_x) is the welfare gain of a

  additive only because the no-savings allocation is closed-form and log-quadratic, whereas in Bewley the shocks interact through the asset distribution and the borrowing constraint.
